Ex 1. Modes of Convergence of Random Variables

a.s. • Probability • L² • Distribution

A sequence of real numbers \(a_n \to a\) has a single meaning. A sequence of random variables \(X_n\) is a sequence of functions \(X_n(\omega)\) on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, P)\), so "\(X_n\) gets close to \(X\)" can be made precise in several non-equivalent ways: pointwise for almost every outcome, with high probability, on average in quadratic distance, or only through their laws. Each mode answers a different question, and the main limit theorems are statements in different modes.

Almost Sure Convergence (a.s.)

$$X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X \iff P\big(\{\omega : \lim_{n\to\infty} X_n(\omega) = X(\omega)\}\big) = 1$$

The strongest pathwise notion: for almost every outcome, the single realised trajectory converges. Equivalent criterion: \(P\big(\sup_{m \ge n} |X_m - X| > \varepsilon\big) \to 0\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), i.e. after some (random) time the sequence never again leaves the band. It is what justifies trusting one long simulation or one long stream of logs.

Convergence in Probability

$$X_n \xrightarrow{P} X \iff \forall \varepsilon > 0:\ P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0$$

At each fixed large \(n\), a large deviation is unlikely, but nothing prevents rare excursions from happening infinitely often along a path. It compares \(P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon)\) with the a.s. quantity \(P(\sup_{m\ge n}|X_m - X| > \varepsilon)\), which is larger. This is the mode of the weak LLN and of consistency of estimators.

Mean-Square Convergence (L²)

$$X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X \iff E\big[(X_n - X)^2\big] \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0$$

Convergence in quadratic mean controls the average size of the error, including its rare large values. For an estimator \(\hat\theta_n\) of \(\theta\), \(E[(\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2] = \text{Var}(\hat\theta_n) + \text{bias}^2\) is the MSE, so L² convergence means vanishing variance and bias. Chebyshev's inequality \(P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \le E[(X_n-X)^2]/\varepsilon^2\) shows L² ⇒ P. More generally \(L^p\) uses \(E|X_n - X|^p\).

Convergence in Distribution (Weak / in Law)

$$X_n \xrightarrow{d} X \iff F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x) \text{ at every continuity point of } F_X$$

Only the laws converge; \(X_n\) and \(X\) need not even live on the same probability space. It is the weakest mode, and the one of the CLT: it says how to compute approximate probabilities (p-values, confidence intervals) for \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma\), not that this quantity settles to a value. Equivalently \(E[g(X_n)] \to E[g(X)]\) for every bounded continuous \(g\) (Portmanteau), or characteristic functions converge (Lévy).

Hierarchy of the Modes

Solid arrows: implications that always hold. Dashed: partial converses. a.s. and L² do not imply each other. L² ⇒ L¹ ⇒ P, and a.s. + uniform integrability ⇒ L¹.

Why the Arrows Cannot Be Reversed

The laboratory below lets you look inside the three classical counterexamples (\(U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) plays the role of the outcome \(\omega\)):

  • Typewriter sequence \(X_n = \mathbf 1\{U \in I_n\}\), with dyadic intervals \(I_n\) of width \(2^{-k}\) sweeping \([0,1]\) again and again: P and L² to 0, but no a.s. convergence (every path keeps hitting 1).
  • Escaping spike \(X_n = n\,\mathbf 1\{U \le 1/n\}\): a.s. (hence P) to 0, but \(E[X_n^2] = n \to \infty\), so no L² convergence.
  • Mirror \(X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1/2)\), \(X_n = 1 - X\): \(X_n \stackrel{d}{=} X\) for every \(n\) (trivially in distribution), yet \(|X_n - X| = 1\) always, so no convergence in probability.

Same Sample Mean, Different Questions

For i.i.d. \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the sample mean \(\bar X_n = S_n/n\) is the object of three different theorems, each in its own mode:

Ex 3. Why √n Appears Everywhere

Additivity of Variance & Independence

The \(\sqrt n\) comes from one identity. For any random variables with finite variance:

$$\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big) = \sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i<j} \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$$
If the \(X_i\) are independent (it suffices that they are uncorrelated) all \(n(n-1)/2\) covariance terms vanish and variances add: \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2\). Standard deviations do not add, they grow like \(\sigma\sqrt n\): independent errors partly cancel each other.

Three normalisations (HMWK 03 revisited)

  • \(S_n\): \(\text{SD} = \sigma\sqrt n \to \infty\), it spreads out.
  • \(S_n / n = \bar X_n\): \(\text{SD} = \sigma/\sqrt n \to 0\), it collapses onto \(\mu\) (LLN).
  • \(S_n/\sqrt n\) (centred): \(\text{SD} = \sigma\) for every \(n\). This is the only scaling that keeps a non-degenerate limit, and the CLT says that limit is Gaussian.

So \(\sqrt n\) is the exact speed at which the LLN error shrinks, and the exact zoom needed to look at that error.

√n in statistical inference

  • Standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt n\); confidence interval \(\bar x \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,s/\sqrt n\). To halve the error you need four times the data.
  • Test statistics \(z = \sqrt n(\bar x - \mu_0)/\sigma\): for a fixed true effect, \(z\) grows like \(\sqrt n\), which is why huge samples make tiny effects "significant" (HMWK 06).
  • \(\sqrt n\)-consistency of MLEs, Berry–Esseen error \(O(1/\sqrt n)\) of the normal approximation, Monte Carlo error \(O(1/\sqrt M)\).
  • Cryptanalysis: distinguishing a bias \(\varepsilon\) from noise needs \(\varepsilon\sqrt N \gtrsim\) const, i.e. \(N \approx 1/\varepsilon^2\) samples (data complexity of linear / differential attacks).

What happens without independence

With positive correlation the covariance terms no longer cancel. For a stationary AR(1) process with lag-one correlation \(\rho\):

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n_{\text{eff}}}, \qquad n_{\text{eff}} = n\,\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}$$

The rate is still \(1/\sqrt n\), but the constant grows: 1000 correlated observations with \(\rho = 0.9\) are worth about 53 independent ones. In the extreme \(\rho = 1\) (all \(X_i\) equal) \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n^2\sigma^2\) and averaging is useless.

Cybersecurity relevance

Security telemetry is rarely i.i.d.: attacks arrive in bursts, failed logins cluster, and alerts from the same campaign are correlated. Computing an alert-rate confidence interval with \(s/\sqrt n\) on bursty logs underestimates the uncertainty and produces false anomalies. Remedies are block bootstrap, batch means, or modelling the dependence explicitly. Heavy tails (e.g. Cauchy-like transfer sizes) break the \(\sqrt n\) law completely, because \(\sigma^2 = \infty\).

Ex 4. Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

Updating Beliefs with Evidence

Conditional probability (probability theory)

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0$$

Conditioning on \(B\) restricts the sample space to \(B\) and renormalises. From the definition follow the multiplication rule \(P(A\cap B) = P(A\mid B)P(B)\) and the law of total probability \(P(B) = \sum_k P(B \mid A_k)P(A_k)\) for a partition \(\{A_k\}\). Independence (HMWK 06) is exactly the case \(P(A\mid B) = P(A)\).

Conditioning in statistics

In statistics conditioning appears as conditional distributions (the row profiles of a contingency table), conditional expectation \(E[Y\mid X]\) (regression), and above all the model \(f(x \mid \theta)\): the distribution of the data given the parameter. Frequentist methods treat \(\theta\) as fixed and reason on \(f(x\mid\theta)\); Bayesian methods also give \(\theta\) a distribution and condition on the observed data.

Bayes' theorem inverts the direction of conditioning. For a hypothesis \(H\) and evidence \(E\):
$$\underbrace{P(H \mid E)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{P(E \mid H)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{P(H)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{P(E)}_{\text{evidence}}}, \qquad P(E) = \sum_k P(E \mid H_k)\,P(H_k)$$
Equivalently, in odds form:
$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H\mid E)}{P(\bar H\mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(E\mid H)}{P(E\mid \bar H)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\bar H)}}_{\text{prior odds}}$$
The odds form is the most useful in practice: evidence multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio, independently of where we started. Confusing \(P(E\mid H)\) with \(P(H\mid E)\) is the prosecutor's fallacy, and ignoring the prior is the base-rate fallacy.

Ex 5. Bayesian Inference: Prior, Likelihood, Posterior

Beta–Binomial Model

Bayesian inference applies Bayes' theorem to an unknown parameter \(\theta\), treated as a random variable that describes our uncertainty:

$$\pi(\theta \mid x) = \frac{L(\theta; x)\,\pi(\theta)}{\int L(\vartheta; x)\,\pi(\vartheta)\,d\vartheta} \;\propto\; L(\theta; x)\,\pi(\theta), \qquad L(\theta; x) = f(x \mid \theta)$$

Prior \(\pi(\theta)\)

What is believed about \(\theta\) before the data: expert knowledge, previous studies, last quarter's results, or deliberate vagueness (flat, Jeffreys). It is a full distribution, so it encodes both a best guess and how confident we are in it.

Likelihood \(L(\theta; x)\)

The probability of the observed data as a function of \(\theta\). It is not a probability distribution over \(\theta\) (it need not integrate to 1). It is the only channel through which the data speak, and it is shared with frequentist inference (MLE = its maximiser).

Posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid x)\)

The updated belief after the data: prior times likelihood, renormalised. All conclusions come from it: point estimates (mean, MAP), credible intervals \(P(\theta \in I \mid x) = 0.95\), probabilities of hypotheses, predictions.

Why updating is central

  • Sequential coherence: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. Updating with \(x_1\) then \(x_2\) gives the same result as updating with \((x_1, x_2)\) at once, which suits streaming security data.
  • Direct probability statements about parameters and hypotheses ("P(click rate > 10% | data) = 0.83"), which a p-value or a frequentist CI cannot give.
  • Decision theory: combined with a loss function, the posterior directly gives optimal actions (block / allow, patch now / later).
  • Asymptotic agreement (Bernstein–von Mises): as \(n\) grows the likelihood dominates any reasonable prior, and the posterior becomes approximately \(\mathcal N(\hat\theta_{MLE}, \sigma^2/n)\). The \(\sqrt n\) from Ex 3 returns as the speed at which the posterior concentrates.

Conjugate example: Beta prior, Binomial likelihood

Let \(\theta\) be the probability that an employee clicks on a phishing e-mail. Prior \(\theta \sim \text{Beta}(a, b)\); data: \(k\) clicks in \(n\) e-mails, \(L(\theta) \propto \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\). Then

$$\theta \mid k \sim \text{Beta}(a + k,\; b + n - k)$$ $$E[\theta \mid k] = \underbrace{\tfrac{n}{n+a+b}}_{w}\cdot\frac{k}{n} + (1-w)\cdot\frac{a}{a+b}$$

The posterior mean is a weighted average of the MLE and the prior mean; \(a+b\) acts as a number of "pseudo-observations" contributed by the prior.

Advantages

  • Uses existing knowledge explicitly and transparently; especially valuable when data are scarce (rare incidents, small teams, new systems).
  • Answers the questions practitioners actually ask: the probability that a hypothesis is true, the probability that a rate exceeds a threshold.
  • Natural sequential updating and coherent handling of uncertainty in predictions and decisions.
  • Shrinkage stabilises estimates from small samples (6/40 is pulled towards the historical 5%, instead of trusting the noisy 15%).

Limitations

  • Prior subjectivity: with little data, the prior can dominate, as the "strong but wrong" prior shows. Sensitivity analysis is mandatory.
  • A wrong likelihood model (e.g. assuming independent alerts or e-mails when they are correlated, Ex 3) gives overconfident posteriors that no prior can fix.
  • Priors that assign zero probability to a value can never be corrected by data (Cromwell's rule).
  • Computational cost: outside conjugate models the normalising integral requires MCMC or variational approximations.
Conclusion. The four modes of convergence describe increasingly weak senses in which randomness "settles". Almost-sure and in-probability convergence underpin the strong and weak LLN (estimators get close to the truth), L² quantifies the error as \(\sigma^2/n\), and convergence in distribution, the CLT, describes its Gaussian shape. All of them depend on the additivity of variance under independence, which makes \(\sqrt n\) the universal rate of statistical learning, and that rate degrades when observations are correlated. Bayes' theorem moves the same reasoning to beliefs: the likelihood carries the data, the prior carries what was known before, and the posterior combines them coherently. With little data the prior matters and must be justified; with many data the likelihood dominates and Bayesian and frequentist conclusions converge, again at speed \(1/\sqrt n\).
