1. Stochastic Penetration Process & Monte Carlo Simulator

n Systems • m Attackers • i.i.d. Increments Xₖ

We model the sequential penetration of n systems by m independent attackers as a discrete-time stochastic process with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) increments X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ. At each step i ∈ {1, …, n}, an attacker attempts to compromise system i, yielding a step score Xₖ under one of two probabilistic regimes:

  • Bernoulli Model (Cumulative Breach Process): Xₖ ∈ {0, 1} with P(Xₖ = 1) = p and P(Xₖ = 0) = 1 − p. Each step represents a binary breach outcome (1 = compromised, 0 = defended), with step expectation μₓ = p and step variance σₓ² = p(1 − p).
  • Rademacher Model (Symmetric Zero-Mean Random Walk): Xₖ ∈ {−1, +1} with P(Xₖ = +1) = P(Xₖ = −1) = 1/2. This models a symmetric tug-of-war between offense (+1) and defensive remediation/pushback (−1), yielding exact zero drift μₓ = 0 and unit step variance σₓ² = 1.
1. Total Score Sₙ = ∑ₖ₁ⁿ Xₖ
2. Average Score (LLN) Sₙ / n = (1 / n) ∑ₖ₁ⁿ Xₖ
3. Normalized Score (CLT) Sₙ / √n = (1 / √n) ∑ₖ₁ⁿ Xₖ

3. Mathematical Comparison of Normalizations, LLN & CLT

Asymptotic Probability Theory

Let X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ be i.i.d. random variables with finite mean μ = E[Xₖ] and finite positive variance σ² = Var(Xₖ). By linearity of expectation and independence of increments, scaling the partial sum Sₙ = ∑ Xₖ by n−α scales the expectation by n1−α and the variance by n1−2α:

1. Convergence in Probability (Law of Large Numbers)

For the sample average Sₙ/n, we have E[Sₙ/n] = μ and Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n. Applying Chebyshev's Inequality for any threshold ε > 0:

P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(Sₙ/n) / ε² = σ² / (nε²) → 0

As n → ∞, the probability that the empirical penetration rate deviates from the true mean μ (p for Bernoulli, 0 for Rademacher) vanishes. Visually, the m trajectories form a narrowing funnel that pinches onto the horizontal line y = μ.

2. Variance Behavior under Normalizations

Because the increments Xₖ are independent, their variances add linearly: Var(Sₙ) = ∑ Var(Xₖ) = nσ². When we multiply Sₙ by a deterministic factor cₙ, the variance scales quadratically by cₙ²:

  • For cₙ = 1: Var(Sₙ) = nσ² → ∞ (unbounded spread).
  • For cₙ = 1/n: Var(Sₙ/n) = (1/n²)nσ² = σ²/n → 0 (degenerate point mass).
  • For cₙ = 1/√n: Var(Sₙ/√n) = (1/n)nσ² = σ² (non-degenerate, stable distribution).

Thus, √n is the unique critical scaling factor that neither blows up the variance nor crushes random fluctuations to zero.

3. Central Limit Theorem in the Rademacher Model

In the symmetric Rademacher model, E[Xₖ] = 0 and Var(Xₖ) = 1. Therefore, the normalized total score is already standardized:

Zₙ = (Sₙ − nμ) / (σ√n) = Sₙ / √n →d N(0, 1)

Using characteristic functions, φSₙ/√n(t) = E[eitX₁/√n]n = [&cos;(t/√n)]n = (1 − t²/(2n) + O(n−2))n → e−t²/2, which is the exact characteristic function of the Standard Normal N(0, 1). By contrast, in the Bernoulli model (μ = p > 0), Sₙ/√n = √n·p + √(p(1−p))Zₙ has constant variance p(1−p) but its center drifts to +∞ at rate √n·p.

4. Cybersecurity Interpretation: n Systems & m Attackers

Threat Modeling • Attack Surface vs. Adversary Population

Role of System Scale (n)

The parameter n represents the horizontal attack surface (number of hosts/endpoints audited by an attacker) or the depth of a multi-stage defense architecture:

  • Predictability of Compromise Rate (LLN): Across a large enterprise infrastructure (large n), the fraction of compromised systems Sₙ/n stabilizes tightly around the intrinsic vulnerability probability p. Security posture metrics measured over large asset inventories have minimal sampling noise (±1/√n).
  • Growth of Absolute Exposure (Total Score Sₙ): In the Bernoulli model, expected compromised assets grow linearly as np, while uncertainty grows as √(np(1−p)). Even in a balanced cyber conflict (Rademacher, zero average drift), an attacker can achieve large temporary excursions of order O(√n) purely by random fluctuation.

Role of Attacker Population (m)

The parameter m represents the number of concurrent threat actors (or automated botnet campaigns) probing the infrastructure simultaneously:

  • Empirical Resolution of Threat Distributions: While a single attacker (m = 1) traces one erratic random path, a large adversary population (large m) populates the entire probability distribution, making the Gaussian bell curve of Sₙ/√n empirically visible.
  • Extreme-Value Tail Risk (Worst-Case Breach): A defender must protect against the most successful attacker among all m adversaries: Mn,m = max1≤j≤m Sₙ(j). Because Sₙ/√n ≈ N(0, 1) in the Rademacher case, extreme value theory for Gaussian maxima implies that as m grows, the worst-case penetration scales as E[max Sₙ] ≈ √n · √(2 ln m). More attackers directly increase the probability of a low-probability, high-impact tail breach.
